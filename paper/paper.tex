\begin{abstract}
Thomason's lollipop algorithm finds a second Hamiltonian cycle in a cubic graph, given a first one.
It is known to take exponentially many steps in the worst case. The best previous lower bound,
due to Bria\'nski and Szady, is $\Omega(1.1812^n)$ on 3-connected planar cubic graphs. We give
3-connected planar cubic graphs with exactly three Hamiltonian cycles on which the algorithm
takes $\Theta(\rho^{n/6}) = \Theta(1.19742^n)$ steps, where $\rho \approx 2.94771$ is the largest
root of $\lambda^4 - 2\lambda^3 - 2\lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 1$. This answers, for this class, the
question of Bria\'nski and Szady whether faster-growing families exist. The bound comes from a
general method. We show that across a 3-edge cut the lollipop walk either behaves exactly as in
the contracted graph or turns back (a \emph{Mirror Lemma}). Each side of such a cut acts as a
finite automaton that does not depend on the rest of the graph. For sides with a unique
Hamiltonian path between every pair of ports, these automata compose by an exact linear
recursion. The growth rate of a nested family is then the spectral radius of a small integer
matrix, computed from a constant-size gadget.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

By Smith's theorem (published by Tutte~\cite{Tutte46}), every edge of a cubic graph lies in an
even number of Hamiltonian cycles. So a cubic graph with one Hamiltonian cycle has another.
Thomason~\cite{Thomason78} gave a constructive proof, the \emph{lollipop algorithm}: starting
from a Hamiltonian cycle and a fixed edge, it walks through a sequence of Hamiltonian paths and
stops at a second Hamiltonian cycle through that edge. Papadimitriou used this argument to define
the class PPA~\cite{Papadimitriou94}. Whether a second Hamiltonian cycle can be found in
polynomial time is open, and so is whether the problem is PPA-complete; see
also~\cite{Eppstein23} for the complexity of computing the specific cycle that the lollipop
algorithm returns.

The lollipop algorithm can take exponentially many steps. Krawczyk~\cite{Krawczyk99} gave the
first exponential family, and Cameron~\cite{Cameron01} corrected and generalised it, with
$2^{\Theta(n)}$ steps on planar graphs. Zhong~\cite{Zhong18} gave cyclically 4-edge-connected
bipartite families with $\Theta(2^{n/16})$ steps. Bria\'nski and Szady~\cite{BrianskiSzady22}
gave 3-connected planar cubic graphs with $\Omega(1.1812^n)$ steps, the best known
base~\cite{BKN24}, and asked whether families with faster growth exist. In the other direction,
the algorithm takes at most $n$ steps on cubic bipartite Pfaffian graphs~\cite{BKN24}. The
fastest known exact algorithm for a second Hamiltonian cycle runs in $O(1.2311^n)$
time~\cite{DMSZ20}.

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $\rho = 2.947711586\ldots$ be the largest real root of
$\lambda^4 - 2\lambda^3 - 2\lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 1$. For every $k \ge 0$ there is a 3-connected
planar cubic graph $G_k$ on $n = 8 + 6k$ vertices with exactly three Hamiltonian cycles, a
Hamiltonian cycle $C_k$ and an edge $v_0v_1$ of $C_k$, such that Thomason's algorithm started
from $(C_k, v_0v_1)$ performs $\Theta(\rho^k)$ steps. Hence it takes
$\Theta(\rho^{n/6}) = \Theta(1.19742^n)$ steps, against the previous best $\Omega(1.1812^n)$.
\end{theorem}

The step counts are exact: $10, 25, 73, 214, 628, 1849, 5449, 16060, \dots$ for
$k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ (Section~\ref{sec:family}). The proof rests on three structural results about
the lollipop walk across a 3-edge cut, which we expect to be useful beyond this construction.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{Mirror Lemma} (Theorem~\ref{thm:mirror}). If $X$ is a side of a 3-edge cut, the
output of the walk on $G$ either projects to the output on $G/X$, or differs from the input cycle
only inside $X$.
\item The \textbf{Transducer Theorem} (Theorem~\ref{thm:transducer}). Each visit of the walk to
$X$ has an outcome (pass through or turn back), a successor state and a cost determined by $X$
alone.
\item The \textbf{Composition Lemma} (Lemma~\ref{lem:composition}). If every pair of ports of $X$
is joined by exactly one Hamiltonian path of $X$, then $X$ never turns the walk back
(Lemma~\ref{lem:simple}), and costs of nested 3-poles satisfy an exact linear recursion.
\end{itemize}
Together these turn the analysis of a nested family into a finite computation on a constant-size
gadget, here a 7-vertex 3-pole, with a hand-checkable certificate (Appendix~\ref{app:visits}).
All computational claims are re-checked by a short standalone script
(Section~\ref{sec:verification}).

\section{Preliminaries}

All graphs are simple. $N(v)$ is the neighbourhood and $N[v] = N(v) \cup \{v\}$; for a vertex
set $X$, $N(X)$ is the set of vertices outside $X$ with a neighbour in $X$, and $\delta(X)$ is
the set of edges with exactly one end in $X$. For a sequence $S$, $\rev(S)$ is its reversal, and
$\cdot$ is concatenation.

\paragraph{The lollipop walk.} Let $G$ be cubic, $C = (v_0, v_1, \dots, v_{n-1})$ a Hamiltonian
cycle, and $v_0v_1$ the fixed edge. A \emph{state} is a Hamiltonian path of $G$ starting with
$v_0v_1$. Let $P$ be a state with endpoint $z$ and let $zw$ be a non-path edge with $w \ne v_0$.
If $s$ is the successor of $w$ on $P$, the \emph{rotation} of $P$ along $zw$ is
$P' = P[v_0..w] \cdot \rev(P[s..z])$, i.e.\ add $zw$ and delete $ws$. The \emph{state graph}
$\cG(G)$ joins $P$ and $P'$; rotations are symmetric, and every state has degree at most $2$.
States whose endpoint is adjacent to $v_0$ have degree at most $1$; they close to Hamiltonian
cycles through $v_0v_1$. The walk starts at the leaf $S_0 = C - v_{n-1}v_0$ and follows its
component of $\cG(G)$, which is therefore a path, to the other leaf. Operationally, a
\emph{step} at endpoint $z$ uses the unique edge $zw$ with $w$ different from the predecessor of
$z$ and from the previous attachment vertex (initially $v_0$). The walk stops when this edge goes
to $v_0$. The number of steps is the number of rotations performed.

\paragraph{3-poles.} A \emph{3-pole} in $G$ is a set $X \subset V(G)$ with $|\delta(X)| = 3$
such that the cut edges $e_i = p_iq_i$ ($p_i \in X$, $i = 1,2,3$) form a matching. The $p_i$ are
the \emph{ports}. $G/X$ denotes the graph with $X$ contracted to one vertex $x$; it is simple and
cubic. Write $h_{ij}(X)$ for the number of Hamiltonian paths of $G[X]$ from $p_i$ to $p_j$. $X$ is
\emph{simple} if $h_{ij}(X) = 1$ for all $i \ne j$. If $r$ is a vertex of a cubic graph $F$, then
$F - r$ is a 3-pole with ports $N_F(r)$ whenever it is placed in a host. For a cubic graph $H$, a
vertex $y$ of $H$ and a 3-pole $P$, $H[y \leftarrow P]$ denotes the graph obtained by replacing
$y$ by $P$, with the three edges at $y$ joined to the ports by a fixed bijection (the
\emph{wiring}).

Throughout we assume the \textbf{standing hypothesis}: $v_0 \notin X \cup N(X)$ (so also
$v_1 \notin X$).

\section{The Mirror Lemma}

Let $Y = V(G) \setminus X$ and $H = G/X$. A state starts in $Y$ and alternates between $Y$- and
$X$-segments; since $|\delta(X)| = 3$, it has exactly one of the following \emph{types}, and we
define $\pi$ accordingly:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llcl}
\toprule
type & shape & cut edges used & $\pi(P)$ \\
\midrule
A  & $Y_1 \cdot X \cdot Y_2$, endpoint in $Y_2$ & 2 & $Y_1 \cdot x \cdot Y_2$ \\
B1 & $Y_1 \cdot X$, endpoint in $X$ & 1 & $Y_1 \cdot x$ \\
B3 & $Y_1 \cdot X_1 \cdot Y_2 \cdot X_2$, endpoint in $X_2$ & 3 & $Y_1 \cdot x \cdot Y_2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each $\pi(P)$ is a state of $H$, because $v_0, v_1 \in Y_1$. In type B3 we write $p_a, p_b, p_c$
for the entry port of $X_1$, the exit port of $X_1$ and the entry port of $X_2$.

\begin{theorem}[Mirror Lemma]\label{thm:mirror}
$\pi$ maps every edge of $\cG(G)$ to an edge of $\cG(H)$ or to a single vertex. Consequently,
the cycle returned on $G$ projects to either the cycle returned on $H$ or to $C/X$; in the second
case it agrees with $C$ outside $X$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $P \to P'$ be the rotation at endpoint $z$ along $zw$, deleting $ws$. Contracting a
contiguous block commutes with suffix reversal as long as the cut point $ws$ is outside the
block; most cases reduce to this remark.

\emph{$P$ of type A ($z \in Y_2$).}
(i) $w, s \in Y$: the $X$-block is untouched or reversed as a whole; $P'$ has type A and $\pi(P')$
is the rotation of $\pi(P)$ along $zw$.
(ii) $w \in Y$, $s \in X$: $ws$ is the entry cut edge, so $w = q_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the last vertex
of $Y_1$; $P' = Y_1 \cdot \rev(Y_2) \cdot \rev(X)$ has type B1, and
$\pi(P') = Y_1 \cdot \rev(Y_2) \cdot x$ is the rotation of $\pi(P)$ along $zw$ (deleting $wx$).
(iii) $w \in X$: then $zw = e_j$ is the unused cut edge ($z = q_j$, $w = p_j$). Since the ports are
distinct, $p_j$ is an interior vertex of the $X$-block, so $s \in X$. Writing $X = X_a \cdot X_b$
with $X_a$ ending at $w$, $P' = Y_1 \cdot X_a \cdot \rev(Y_2) \cdot \rev(X_b)$ has type B3 and
$\pi(P') = Y_1 \cdot x \cdot \rev(Y_2)$. This is the rotation of $\pi(P)$ along the non-path edge
$q_jx$, which deletes the edge from $x$ to the first vertex of $Y_2$.

\emph{$P$ of type B1.}
(i) $w \in X$: then $s \in X$, the $Y$-part is unchanged, and $\pi(P') = \pi(P)$.
(ii) $w \in Y$: then $z = p_j$ and $w = q_j$ with $j \ne \mathrm{in}$. As the $q$'s are distinct,
$w$ is not the last vertex of $Y_1$, so $s \in Y_1$. Writing $Y_1 = Y_a \cdot Y_b$ with $Y_a$ ending
at $w$, $P' = Y_a \cdot \rev(X) \cdot \rev(Y_b)$ has type A, and $\pi(P') = Y_a \cdot x \cdot
\rev(Y_b)$ is the rotation of $\pi(P) = Y_1 \cdot x$ along $xq_j$, where $q_j \ne v_0$ by the
standing hypothesis.

\emph{$P$ of type B3.} All three cut edges are path edges, so $w \in X$.
(i) $w \in X_2$: then $s \in X_2$ and $\pi(P') = \pi(P)$.
(ii) $w \in X_1$, $w \ne p_b$: then $s \in X_1$; writing $X_1 = X_{1a} \cdot X_{1b}$,
$P' = Y_1 \cdot (X_{1a} \cdot \rev(X_2)) \cdot \rev(Y_2) \cdot \rev(X_{1b})$ has type B3.
(iii) $w = p_b$: then $s = q_b$ and $P' = Y_1 \cdot (X_1 \cdot \rev(X_2)) \cdot \rev(Y_2)$ has type A.
In cases (ii) and (iii), $\pi(P') = Y_1 \cdot x \cdot \rev(Y_2)$. Since $\pi(P) = Y_1 \cdot x \cdot
Y_2$ ends at $q_c$ and $q_cx$ is not a path edge of $\pi(P)$, this is the rotation of $\pi(P)$
along $q_cx$.

No other case occurs: a non-path edge leaves $X$ or enters $X$ only along an unused cut edge.

For the consequence: the walk on $G$ follows the component of $\cG(G)$ from $S_0(G)$ to its other
leaf $T$, so its image is a connected subgraph of the component $L$ of $\cG(H)$ containing
$\pi(S_0(G)) = S_0(H)$. $L$ is a path whose two leaves close to $C/X$ and to the output on $H$.
The endpoint of $T$ is adjacent to $v_0$, hence lies in $Y$ by the standing hypothesis, so $T$
has type A and $\pi(T)$ is a leaf of $\cG(H)$ in $L$.
\end{proof}

\section{Visits and the Transducer Theorem}

A \emph{visit} to $X$ is a maximal run of the walk during which the endpoint lies in $X$,
together with the move that starts it (the \emph{entry move}) and the move that ends it (the
\emph{exit move}). By the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:mirror}, a visit starts from a state of type A
whose $X$-segment is a Hamiltonian path $Q$ of $X$, oriented from the port nearer $v_0$. There are
two kinds of visit:
\begin{description}
\item[B1] the entry move deletes the entry cut edge at $Q$'s first port (case A(ii));
\item[B3] the entry move adds the unused cut edge at the third port (case A(iii)).
\end{description}
The \emph{cost} of a B1 visit is the number of moves strictly between its entry and exit moves.
The cost of a B3 visit is the number of moves after its entry move, up to and including its exit
move. In both cases this equals the number of moves the walk on $G$ makes in addition to the
walk on $H$ (where the passage through $x$ takes two moves or one move, respectively). A visit
\emph{transmits} if the projected walk continues after the visit as the walk on $H$ does, and
\emph{reflects} otherwise.

\begin{theorem}[Transducer Theorem]\label{thm:transducer}
For every 3-pole $X$, oriented Hamiltonian path $Q$ of $X$ between two ports, and kind
$\kappa \in \{\mathrm{B1}, \mathrm{B3}\}$, there are an outcome (transmit or reflect), a
successor path $Q'$ and a cost $c_X(Q, \kappa)$ such that every visit of kind $\kappa$ starting
with $X$-segment $Q$, in any host satisfying the standing hypothesis, has this outcome, leaves
$X$-segment $Q'$, and has this cost. Moreover:
\begin{enumerate}
\item outside visits, the walk on $G$ projects step by step onto the walk on $H$;
\item after a transmitting visit, the walk on $G$ is at a lift of the state reached by the walk
on $H$, with the same previous attachment vertex (a port being identified with $x$);
\item a reflecting B1 visit ends at a lift of the state preceding the entry move, and a
reflecting B3 visit at a lift of the state before the entry move.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Host independence.} While the endpoint is in $X$, a move is determined by the endpoint
$z$, its neighbours, its predecessor and the previous attachment vertex. In type B1 states every
attachment lies in $X$, its successor lies in $X$, and only the $X$-segment changes (case B1(i))
until an exit along an unused cut edge (case B1(ii)). In type B3 states the moves are B3(i), B3(ii)
(which re-splits the $X$-pieces and reverses $Y_2$ as an opaque block) and the exit B3(iii). So
the sequence of $X$-pieces, with their order and orientation, evolves as a function of $Q$ and
$\kappa$ alone. After the first internal move the previous attachment lies in $X$; at the first
move of a B1 visit it is $q_{\mathrm{in}}$, so the visit cannot leave through $p_{\mathrm{in}}$
immediately.

\emph{Item 1.} Outside visits, $z \in Y$. In $G$ and in $H$ the edges at $z$, the predecessor of
$z$ and the previous attachment (with ports identified with $x$) correspond, so the admissible
edge is the same and the move projects to the move of the walk on $H$ (cases A(i)--(iii)).

\emph{Items 2 and 3, B1.} The entry move is the move $S \to S^*$ of the walk on $H$ that makes
$x$ the endpoint by deleting $q_{\mathrm{in}}x$, after which the previous attachment is
$q_{\mathrm{in}}$. The exit is a rotation along a cut edge $p_jq_j$ and projects to the rotation of
$S^*$ along $xq_j$ (case B1(ii)), after which the previous attachment is $q_j$ in both graphs. The
walk on $H$ at $S^*$ uses the edge $xq_c$ to the third port's neighbour. So exiting through $p_c$
is that move (transmit), and exiting through $p_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the rotation along
$xq_{\mathrm{in}}$, which undoes the entry move and returns to $S$ (reflect).

\emph{Items 2 and 3, B3.} The entry move is the move $S \to S^*$ of the walk on $H$ that attaches
$q_j$ to $x$; the previous attachment becomes $x$. Moves B3(ii) project alternately to $S$ and
$S^*$, and the exit B3(iii) lands at a lift of $Y_1 \cdot x \cdot \rev(Y_2)$, one of $S, S^*$, with
previous attachment $p_b \mapsto x$. At $S^*$ the walk on $H$ continues from there (transmit). At
$S$ the endpoint $q_j$ has non-path edges $q_jx$ (forbidden) and one other, so the walk proceeds
backwards along $L$ (reflect).

The cost is the number of moves of the visit, which is determined by the first part.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[simple poles are transparent]\label{lem:simple}
If $X$ is simple, every visit to $X$ transmits.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A state of $H$ of type A (its endpoint is not $x$, and $x$ is interior) has exactly one lift to
$G$: its $Y$-part is fixed, and its $X$-segment is the unique Hamiltonian path of $X$ between the
two ports it uses. By Theorem~\ref{thm:transducer}(3), a reflecting visit returns the walk to the
lift of the state before its entry move, which is of type A. That state is the one the walk
occupied before the visit, so the walk on $G$ would revisit a state. But it follows a path in
$\cG(G)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Composition]\label{lem:composition}
Let $P$ be a 3-pole and $X \subset P$ a simple 3-pole with $N(X) \subseteq P$, and let
$P' = P/X$, with $X$ contracted to $x$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $h_{ij}(P) = h_{ij}(P')$ for all port pairs;
\item[(b)] $P$ and $P'$ have the same outcomes and successors, under the bijection of (a);
\item[(c)] $c_P(Q, \kappa) = c_{P'}(Q/X, \kappa) + \sum_f M_{(Q/X, \kappa), f} \; c_X(f)$,
where $M_{(Q', \kappa), f}$ is the number of \emph{passages} of kind $f$ through $x$ during the
visit $(Q', \kappa)$ to $P'$. A passage of kind B1 is a move that makes $x$ the endpoint by
deleting $x$'s entry edge; a passage of kind B3 is a move attaching to $x$. In both cases the
passage records the oriented pair of ports of $X$ used by the path at that moment.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) A Hamiltonian path of $P$ between two ports crosses $\delta(X)$ exactly twice, so it is a
Hamiltonian path of $P'$ together with a Hamiltonian path of $X$ between the corresponding ports.
The latter is unique.

(b), (c) Fix a host $H_0[y \leftarrow P]$ satisfying the standing hypothesis. Since
$v_0 \notin P \cup N(P)$ and $N(X) \subseteq P$, the standing hypothesis also holds for $X$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:simple} and Theorem~\ref{thm:transducer}, the walk on $G = H_0[y \leftarrow P]$
is the walk on $G' = G/X = H_0[y \leftarrow P']$, with each passage through $x$ replaced by a
transmitting visit to $X$. That visit contributes $c_X(f)$ extra moves, and its starting
$X$-segment is forced by (a).

Every passage lies inside a visit to $P'$. A B3 passage starts with the endpoint in
$N(x) \subseteq P'$ and ends with the endpoint in $N(x)$. A B1 passage either occurs inside a
visit, or its move is the entry move of the visit (when the entry deletes the edge from a port
to $x$). Its last move, the rotation at $x$, may coincide with the visit's exit move. In all cases
the extra moves lie strictly inside the window of the corresponding visit to $P$. Hence visits to
$P$ in $G$ and to $P'$ in $G'$ correspond one-to-one with the same kind, starting state up to
contraction, outcome and successor, and their costs differ by the inserted costs. The passages
during a visit to $P'$ are determined by that visit (Theorem~\ref{thm:transducer}), so $M$ does
not depend on the host.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Lemma~\ref{lem:composition} applies only to visit kinds that occur in some walk. In
Section~\ref{sec:family} we only use kinds reachable from a kind that occurs, so this is no
restriction.
\end{remark}

\section{The construction}\label{sec:family}

\paragraph{The base graph.} Let $K$ be the cubic graph on $\{0,\dots,7\}$ formed by the cycle
$0\,1\,2\cdots7\,0$ and the chords $\{0,4\}, \{1,3\}, \{2,6\}, \{5,7\}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:K}). It is 3-connected and planar, and has exactly three Hamiltonian cycles. Let
$x = 5$ and $r = 2$; note $r \notin N_K[x] = \{4,5,6,7\}$. Name the neighbours of $r$ in $K$ as
ports $a = 1$, $b = 3$, $c = 6$. The \emph{wiring} joins the neighbours $4, 6, 7$ of $x$ to the
ports $c, a, b$, respectively.

\paragraph{The family.} Let $G_0 = K$ and $P_0 = K - r$. For $k \ge 1$ let
$G_k = K[x \leftarrow P_{k-1}]$ with the wiring above, and $P_k = G_k - r$, where $r$ always
denotes the copy of $2$ in the outermost copy of $K$. Then $|V(G_k)| = 8 + 6k$, and since
$r \notin N_K[x]$,
\[
  P_k = (K - r)[x \leftarrow P_{k-1}] = P_0[x \leftarrow P_{k-1}], \qquad
  P_k / P_{k-1} = P_0 .
\]
$C_k$ is the Hamiltonian cycle of $G_k$ obtained from $0\,1\,\cdots\,7\,0$ by replacing $x$ with
the unique Hamiltonian path of $P_{k-1}$ between the ports wired to $4$ and $6$. The walk starts
at $v_0 = 0$ with fixed edge $v_0v_1 = \{0,1\}$ (outermost copies).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:class}
Every $G_k$ is a 3-connected planar cubic graph with exactly three Hamiltonian cycles, and every
$P_k$ is simple.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Simplicity and three cycles.} $P_0$ is simple (Table~\ref{tab:P0}). If $P_{k-1}$ is simple,
Lemma~\ref{lem:composition}(a) applied to $P_{k-1} \subset P_k$, with $P_k / P_{k-1} = P_0$, gives
that $P_k$ is simple. A Hamiltonian cycle of $G_k = K[x \leftarrow P_{k-1}]$ consists of a
Hamiltonian cycle of $K$, using a pair of edges at $x$, together with the Hamiltonian path of
$P_{k-1}$ between the corresponding ports. $K$ has three Hamiltonian cycles; by Smith's theorem
each edge lies in exactly two of them, so each pair of edges at $x$ is used by exactly one. Hence
$G_k$ has exactly three Hamiltonian cycles.

\emph{3-connectivity.} For cubic graphs this is equivalent to 3-edge-connectivity. Let $F$ be an
edge cut of $K' = K[x \leftarrow P]$ with $|F| \le 2$, where $P = G - r$ for a 3-edge-connected
cubic $G$. If $V(P)$ lies on one side, contracting it gives a cut of size $|F|$ in $K$. Otherwise
let $F_P = F \cap E(P)$. Placing $r$ on either side of the induced partition of $G$ gives cuts of
sizes $|F_P| + \#\text{ports on the other side}$, both at least $3$. Adding them gives
$2|F_P| + 3 \ge 6$, so $|F_P| = 2 = |F|$. Then each port is on the same side as its neighbour in $K$,
and placing $x$ on either side gives cuts of $K$ of sizes equal to the numbers of ports on each
side. Both are at least 3, which is impossible with 3 ports.

\emph{Planarity.} Deleting $r$ from a plane embedding of $G_{k-1}$ leaves a face meeting the ports
in the cyclic order of $r$'s rotation. Deleting $x$ from a plane embedding of $K$ leaves a face
meeting $N_K(x)$ in the cyclic order of $x$'s rotation. Any bijection between 3-element sets
either preserves or reverses cyclic order, and reversal is handled by mirroring the embedding of
$P_{k-1}$. So $P_{k-1}$ can be placed in that face and joined without crossings.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:recursion}
For all $k \ge 1$, the outcome table of $P_k$ equals that of $P_0$, and $c_k = c_0 + M c_{k-1}$,
where $c_k$ is the cost vector of $P_k$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $k$, applying Lemma~\ref{lem:composition} to $X = P_{k-1} \subset P = P_k$, which is
allowed because $P_{k-1}$ is simple (Lemma~\ref{lem:class}) and $N(P_{k-1}) = N_K(x) \subseteq P_k$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.} Apply the Transducer Theorem to $X = P_{k-1}$ in $G_k$
with $H = K$; the standing hypothesis holds since $0 \notin N_K[x]$. The walk on $K$ from
$(0, \{0,1\})$ takes 4 steps and passes through $x$ once, a passage of kind ca/B3. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:simple} the visit transmits, so
\[
  \operatorname{steps}(G_k) = 4 + c_{k-1}(\mathrm{ca/B3}).
\]
Let $R$ be the set of visit kinds reachable from ca/B3 in the directed graph of $M$; it has 10
elements, and $M_R$ has the same spectral radius as $M$. The characteristic polynomial of $M$ is
\[
\lambda^2(\lambda-1)^2(\lambda+1)^2(\lambda^2+1)(\lambda^4-2\lambda^3-2\lambda^2-2\lambda-1),
\]
with the quartic irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, so $\rho(M) = \rho$. For the lower bound, there
is a rational vector $u > 0$ on $R$ with $M_R u \ge \frac{29477}{10000}\, u$ componentwise
(checked in exact arithmetic, Section~\ref{sec:verification}). With
$\varepsilon = \min_i c_0(i)/u_i > 0$ and $M \ge 0$, induction gives $c_k \ge \varepsilon\,
(29477/10000)^k u$ on $R$. For the upper bound, $c_k = \sum_{j \le k} M^j c_0 = O(\rho^k)$, since
$\rho$ is a simple root and the other eigenvalues have modulus less than $\rho$. Hence
$\operatorname{steps}(G_k) = \Theta(\rho^k)$, and $\rho^{1/6} = 1.197421\ldots$. \qed

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verification}

The supplementary script \texttt{verify.py} (standard library only, under a second to run)
re-derives every computational ingredient from the definitions above:
\begin{itemize}
\item $K$ has three Hamiltonian cycles and $P_0$ is simple;
\item all twelve visits of $P_0$ transmit, with the costs and passages of Table~\ref{tab:P0};
\item the certificate $M_R u \ge \frac{29477}{10000} u$, in rational arithmetic;
\item end to end: $G_k$ is built explicitly, Thomason's algorithm is run from $(0,\{0,1\})$, and
the step count is compared with $4 + c_{k-1}(\mathrm{ca/B3})$ for $k \le 10$, i.e.\
$10, 25, 73, 214, 628, 1849, 5449, 16060, 47338, 139537$;
\item for small $k$: cubic, exactly three Hamiltonian cycles (exhaustive search) and
3-edge-connectivity.
\end{itemize}
We also checked the ingredients independently of this script. Two other implementations of the
algorithm agree with it: an edge-set implementation, and a brute-force traversal of the full
state graph. The Mirror Lemma and the Transducer Theorem were tested on thousands of random hosts
and 3-poles, Lemma~\ref{lem:composition} on $71{,}484$ random pairs $(P', X)$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:simple} on $7{,}233$ random simple poles, without exception. When $X$ is not simple,
the conclusions of Lemma~\ref{lem:composition} fail in most cases, so the hypothesis is needed.

\section{Discussion}

The method computes the exact growth rate of any nested family $G_k = K[x \leftarrow G_{k-1} - r]$
with $G_{k-1} - r$ simple from the $(|K|-1)$-vertex gadget $K - r$ alone (here 7 vertices). This makes systematic searches over larger $K$, several substituted vertices, or
substitution of different gadgets cheap. We expect that larger bases can be found this way.
The Mirror Lemma and the Transducer Theorem also describe 3-edge cuts as reversible finite-state
gadgets that pass or reflect the walk, which connects the lollipop walk to zero-player reversible
motion planning~\cite{DHHL22}. This may be a route to Eppstein's question~\cite{Eppstein23}
whether computing the output of the lollipop algorithm is FP$^{\textsf{PSPACE}}$-complete.

\paragraph{Optimality within the design space.} The method makes it cheap to search over
nested families exhaustively. Among all base graphs $K$ with at most 14 vertices and exactly three
Hamiltonian cycles (1, 3, 7 and 24 graphs on 8, 10, 12 and 14 vertices, up to cospectrality), all
choices of $x$, $r \notin N[x]$, wiring and start, no single-gadget family beats the base
$1.197423$; the best 14-vertex family is two levels of ours. Periodic mixtures of up to eight
gadgets on at most 12 vertices (1{,}392 distinct gadget matrices) give no improvement either.
Families whose poles are not simple, so that they reflect the walk, are outside the exact
theory; simulations of several thousand such families with $|K| \in \{10,12\}$ gave at most
about $1.189$ per vertex.

\paragraph{Open problems.} Is there a matching upper bound on the length of the lollipop walk in
terms of a fixed base below $2$? Which bases are achievable with simple nested gadgets? Is the walk
polynomial on cyclically 5-edge-connected cubic graphs?
